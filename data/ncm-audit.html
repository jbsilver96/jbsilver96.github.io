Next Concept Modeling: findings and open questions

Data Desk research report | 23 October 2026

Summary

Our initial CPU evaluation is finished. Its strongest independently checked finding is narrow: rescoring 128 supplied XSum summaries reproduces their reported ROUGE scores. Sudoku and LM1B provide additional reported findings, not independently rescored reproductions. This work does not demonstrate useful code generation or a tenfold energy advantage over autoregressive language models.

What NCM is

The October 2026 Next Concept Modeling system learns discrete, multiscale text representations. Its generator predicts successive refinement levels, processing positions within each level in parallel. A decoder reconstructs text. Fewer sequential steps are potentially valuable, but codec, generator and decoding costs all count.

Paper: arxiv.org/html/…

Pinned implementation: github.com/HazyResearch/ncm/tree/c70274273f48…

The paper’s generation-speed comparisons against diffusion baselines are not evidence of a tenfold energy advantage over frontier LLMs or a matched autoregressive coding baseline. Latency, throughput and joules per useful answer are different quantities.

Scope and evidence

We reviewed paper version 1 and the pinned implementation, distinguishing execution-team reports from independent checks of supplied artifacts. This was inference evaluation and an implementation audit, not training. Matching hashes establishes consistency with recorded content; rescoring establishes the scores of supplied outputs. Neither alone authenticates checkpoint execution.

XSum: independently verified scoring

The evaluation used the first 128 test examples, reduced from a 256-example target through a timing-based fallback. Independent rescoring used rouge-score 0.1.2 with stemming enabled:

  • ROUGE-1: 34.7280, rounding to 34.73.
  • ROUGE-2: 12.2171, rounding to 12.22.
  • ROUGE-L: 27.8899, rounding to 27.89.

Paper comparison values: 35.0 / 12.8 / 28.0.

All 256 hypothesis/reference text hashes and all 384 rounded per-document scores matched. IDs covered 0–127 exactly once. The reference-file hash matched official metadata:

huggingface.co/datasets/hazyresearch/ncm-datasets/blob/…/reference_summaries.pickle

Scoring implementation: github.com/google-research/google-research/…/rouge

Verdict: small-subset numerical agreement. ROUGE-L’s sample SD was 13.435; its approximate normal 95% interval was 25.56–30.22, wider than the relative ±5% band around 28.0 (26.60–29.40). The mean passes the frozen primary numerical screen, but equivalence is not established. The first 128 examples are not a random sample, so the interval does not establish full-test representativeness. Summaries were not independently regenerated.

Sudoku and LM1B: reported findings

The execution team reported 2,000 puzzles per Sudoku difficulty, 6,000 total:

  • Easy: 1,988/2,000, or 99.40%; paper 99.5%.
  • Medium: 1,928/2,000, or 96.40%; paper 96.5%.
  • Hard: 1,726/2,000, or 86.30%; paper 86.1%.

Verdict: reported full-set quality agreement. These outputs were not independently rescored in this audit.

The small LM1B checkpoint reportedly generated 256 samples with seed 42, yielding GPT-2-large FLM perplexity 89.34 and entropy 4.3128; paper comparisons are 93.05 and 4.31. Checkpoint hashes matched official metadata:

huggingface.co/hazyresearch/ncm-checkpoints/blob/…/generator.pt

huggingface.co/hazyresearch/ncm-checkpoints/blob/…/tokenizer.pt

Perplexity and entropy were not independently recomputed from the raw tokens. Lower perplexity is not demonstrated improvement over the paper.

A planned second seed was dropped for budget reasons. The recorded justification did not satisfy the frozen trigger of seed-42 generation alone projecting above 120 minutes. This is a disclosed budget-driven protocol deviation, not successful completion of the planned multiseed check.

Verdict: reported single-seed check.

Generation-call accounting

Execution-team instrumentation reported 12 transformer-stack passes for Sudoku, nine for XSum and 11 for LM1B. Static code inspection is consistent with these counts but does not authenticate the execution logs.

LM1B samples its initial representation level externally, then performs 11 transformer passes. Interpreting the paper’s 12 steps as representation levels rather than transformer calls is our interpretation, not a verified author explanation. XSum’s nine passes differ from the table’s eight; the author acknowledged that discrepancy:

github.com/HazyResearch/ncm/issues/9#…

Pass counts do not measure total latency or energy. Work per pass, conditioning, codec decoding and memory movement also matter.

Execution limitations

The execution report describes approximately 6.3 elapsed hours on a shared two-core CPU system, plus setup. Restarts required partitioned reruns; jobs overlapped, and interrupted work and retries consumed resources. No incremental paid compute was reported. This is not zero economic cost or a clean timing comparison. Generated outputs may vary with precision, kernels and batch composition.

Paper GPU speed claims: NOT VALIDATED by this run.

Energy savings: NOT MEASURED; no suitable energy telemetry.

Natural-language-to-code capability: NOT TESTED.

What remains untested and why

Conditional generation exists in the released XSum and WMT14 paths. It would be incorrect to claim that NCM generally cannot accept conditioning. Those task-specific models do not establish an instruction-following code generator.

At inspection, the official checkpoint repository’s OpenWebText directory contained a placeholder rather than released weights. Its published configuration also used a one-token prefix with unconditional evaluation, not arbitrary multi-token code instructions. These are dated observations about the inspected release:

huggingface.co/hazyresearch/ncm-checkpoints/tree/main

At the pinned commit, training-related model components and losses existed, and the paper supplied objectives and hyperparameters. A complete tested training entry point and end-to-end data/training pipeline for our proposed code task were not available. Training readiness was preliminary, not a guarantee that training could start immediately.

A proposed next gate, not an approved run

First test whether a code-specific codec preserves function behavior. Split and deduplicate data before fitting, train only on the training partition, and separately report held-out exact reconstruction, syntax/AST preservation and functional-test survival. Exact text recovery is not a hard ceiling on semantic correctness: different programs can behave identically.

If that gate succeeds and execution is approved, compare a small conditional NCM against a competent autoregressive baseline with controlled data exposure and complete-system accounting. Include codec training and inference costs, failures, truncations and any retries. Use restricted execution sandboxes.

Freeze correctness and efficiency criteria before execution. Measure pass@1 with uncertainty, batch-one latency, throughput and joules per correct solution on the same hardware. Report training and inference energy separately. Do not turn a speed comparison into an energy claim or extrapolate a small pilot into a scaling law.

Reproducibility and artifact availability

The linked primary paper, pinned code and official releases are public. At report preparation, the audit-specific outputs, logs and manifests were not available as a public downloadable bundle. The independent audit used supplied text artifacts; readers cannot yet reproduce every audit-specific check from this page alone. Any later artifact release should be linked explicitly with versions/checksums and appropriate dataset attribution. A local filename or private chat is not a public download.

Conclusion

The supplied XSum outputs withstand independent rescoring. Sudoku closely agrees with paper results as reported; LM1B is a single-seed check with a disclosed deviation. This is a useful initial evaluation, not an end-to-end reproduction of every claim. Useful coding performance, training economics and energy advantages over autoregressive models remain open questions. A bounded code-feasibility experiment is a proposed next step, subject to a working implementation and a separately approved resource scope.
