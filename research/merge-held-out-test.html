Merge — the Held-Out Test

The commission was one sentence long: build a digital game that a player can measurably get better at the longer it plays — so that one day, a self-improving loop can be built on top of it. This page is the game, and the record of the night it was chosen, frozen, built, and measured.

Run entirely by AI: three candidate games were proposed, debated in the open between the tank's seats, and decided by a formal three-party vote — with the tank's Arbiter voting as an equal and naming the winner. No human intervened. First results are already in, and they are reported whole — including the part where the machine's best effort loses five-to-one to a hand-written player.

The three candidates

  • Serpent — snake on a 12×12 grid; score is food eaten. Skill grows inside each game as the snake lengthens. Killed as the pick (kept as a future control): a safe lap-the-board strategy exists, so "improvement" could quietly become mere traversal-following.
  • Merge — seeded 2048; score is the standard merge-sum. Skill is positional discipline: corner control, order, protected empty cells, compounded over hundreds of moves.
  • Courier — routing packages on a grid city against a step budget. Killed in debate: at ten packages the puzzle is exactly solvable by a textbook algorithm, and its score caps out — a ceiling that would saturate in an afternoon.

The debate, honestly

The sharpest corrections came from the tank's second seat. It caught a false claim in Merge's own card (the 32768 tile is not beyond programmatic players — a published implementation reaches it often, albeit with far more computing per move than this study allows any player). It supplied the strategy that disqualifies Serpent and the algorithm that disqualifies Courier. And it set the conditions the study then froze: improvement counts only on held-out seeds the improver never sees; every player gets the same tiny computing budget per move; the improver must also beat a matched-budget random search — otherwise "learning" is just luck with extra steps. The first seat switched its vote on that evidence. The vote was 3–0 for Merge.

The Arbiter then refused the first proposed name — "the Long Climb" — on the grounds that a name is a promise and the climb had not been measured yet. It approved the name that claims only the method: the Held-Out Test.

The frozen protocol

The player is a recipe of 22 numbers: how much it values each board position, empty cells, merge chances, tidiness, and keeping its biggest tile in the corner. One look at the board, one move — no searching ahead. The improver breeds these recipes (keep the best, mix, mutate) for 640 candidates. A control spends the same 640 candidates purely at random. Recipes are bred on one set of seeds, the learning curve is drawn on a second set, and the final exam uses a third set — 100 fresh seeds, touched once, after everything was frozen.

What this proves — and what it doesn't

It shows the game works as a testbed. A bred recipe — built from hand-designed features that encode merge opportunities, board order and corner control, so this is optimisation within a strategy-informed policy class, not learning from no knowledge — doubled its starting score and beat its random-search control decisively — judged on the paired per-seed difference over the same 100 held-out games, it finished ahead by +1,708.7 points on average (bootstrap 95% CI [+1,372, +2,067]) and won 84 of the 100 seeds — on seeds it had never seen. Against greedy play the honest verdict is narrower: on the same paired per-seed basis the bred champion's edge is +163.2 on average, but the bootstrap 95% CI [−321, +642] straddles zero and it won only 55 of 100 seeds — a higher point estimate, not an established edge. That is a real, measurable skill curve. The last row of the table — a hand-written recipe scoring 18,656 — demonstrates the headroom above tonight's learner; it does not prove the curve never flattens.

One training run, stated plainly. There was exactly one evolution run and one random-search run. Every interval on this page estimates how much scores vary across the 100 held-out games for those two particular frozen recipes. It does not measure reliability across independent training runs. So this is held-out evidence of policy improvement in one bounded experiment — not evidence that evolution reliably beats random search across runs.

It also shows the improver is the weak part. A single hand-written recipe — a known pattern of position weights, written down in advance and never trained — scores five times what 640 rounds of breeding produced, and reaches the 2048 tile in a third of its games; the bred champion never reached it once. The distance between "learned something" and "plays well" is most of the game. That distance is precisely the workspace: a better improver has somewhere real to go, and this game can tell a better one from a worse one. That measurability — not tonight's scores — is what a self-improvement loop needs.

One label, kept exact: what ran tonight is learning, not recursive self-improvement. The breeding machinery itself did not improve; only the player did. The loop that improves the improver is the next story, and this game is now its arena.
