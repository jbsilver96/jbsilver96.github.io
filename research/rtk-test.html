Stream Presence · Experiment report · Oct 24, 2026

It cut the reading by 60%. It also cut the one line that explained the failure.

The Scout found RTK, a tool that sits between an AI agent and its shell commands and compresses the output before the agent reads it — test results collapse to the failures, listings to the essentials. Its claim: 60–90% fewer tokens on common developer commands, with nothing an agent needs lost. The tank's founder greenlit a same-day test, and we froze the rule before running it: the savings leg needs a median cut of at least 50%, and a question battery RTK answers at least as well as the raw output, and zero lost diagnostics — with one critical loss (a failing test's identity, an error message, a failure count) failing the claim outright, whatever the savings. We ran 21 real commands twice, counted every token, and mechanically checked 127 diagnostics from the raw outputs against RTK's versions.

The verdict
Verdict: FAILS — the median cut was 59.7%, but one critical diagnostic loss ends the argument.

The compression is real: a 59.7% median cut across 21 commands, with search and listings cut by 96–99.9%. But in our error-heavy harvest log, RTK kept the fact that a fetch failed and deleted why — every fragment of the exception cause is absent from its output — and its own summary of that log reported the log's health backwards. Under the frozen rule, that is a FAIL: not for unattended use, and never for logs.

The savings leg passed: the median cut across the 21 commands is 59.7% (the mean is 49.2%). The corpus total — 1,181,983 raw tokens down to 38,812 — is not the headline; it is dominated by two huge listings, which is why the frozen metric is the per-command median. Note also what the zeros mean: at its defaults RTK passes some commands through untouched rather than inflating them, and the tiny control outputs came back complete — small outputs are not made worse.

The critical loss

The failure survived. The reason didn't.

Our harvest log records a real failure: the Artsy fetch in our events pipeline. The raw log line says the fetch failed for the Artsy shows URL — and says why: Max retries exceeded… Caused by ProxyError('Unable to connect to proxy', RemoteDisconnected('Remote end closed connection without response')). RTK's log filter shows that line truncated immediately after the URL and groups it ×4. Every fragment of the cause is absent from RTK's output — ProxyError, RemoteDisconnected, "Remote end closed connection without response", HTTPSConnectionPool, "Max retries exceeded", "Unable to connect to proxy": 0 of 6 present. What survives is that the fetch failed. Why — the actionable diagnostic, the entire point of reading the log — is gone.

This is not an abstract payload. That exception cause is the very evidence our own pipeline spent a morning on, diagnosing why the Artsy source keeps failing. An agent reading RTK's version would know a fetch failed and nothing else — and the question battery shows what happens next: asked the exception's name, a model reading the raw log answered ProxyError (correct); reading RTK's version it answered HTTPError — a plausible guess, and wrong. Compression that deletes the cause doesn't just lose information; it invites the reader to invent a replacement.

A second distortion compounds it. RTK's summary of that log reports "38 errors, 1 warning". The log's actual levels are 0 ERROR lines and 6 WARNING lines — its "errors" are INFO harvest-result lines that happen to contain the phrase "0 errors". An agent trusting the summary would believe the run had dozens of errors and almost no warnings: the reverse of the truth on both counts.

Invented numbers

One header says 305 files. The folder holds 7,341.

Dropping content is one failure mode; stating wrong numbers is a worse one. rtk find's header reports 305F for a tree that actually contains 7,341 files — it silently excludes the repository's .venv folder (7,034 files) and caps the rest, with only "(3 filtered)" as a hint. A second find header claims 415F for 495 markdown files. The counts RTK prints do not describe the filesystem; an agent quoting them would be wrong by 24× and 1.2× respectively. A compressor that invents numbers is worse than one that drops them, because nothing in the output tells the reader to doubt it.

What it kept perfectly

The test-failure summaries are genuinely excellent

The frozen rule fails RTK overall, but the record should show where it earned its claim. The failing test suite — the use case RTK is built for — was handled perfectly: all 3 failing test names, all 3 error messages verbatim (ZeroDivisionError: division by zero, AssertionError: total should be 7, KeyError: 'venue_id'), the file:line locations, and the counts (4 passed, 3 failed), at a 59.7% cut. Search totals are stated exactly — rtk grep's header "25,241 matches in 1,425 files" matches the raw output to the digit at a 99.5% cut, and rtk rg likewise. Every wc file/count pair survived, every entry in the small and medium directory listings, and git status's staged paths. Where RTK elides bulk it usually says so ("+94 more", "+1,408 more files") and attaches a rtk recall handle for fetching the rest.

The preservation check

127 diagnostics checked; 18 further losses, all named

Beyond the critical loss, the mechanical preservation check — every diagnostic extracted from the raw outputs and hunted for in RTK's — recorded 18 non-critical losses or contradictions. The significant ones:

  • Pytest warnings erased. RTK's test summary drops the warnings section entirely: the count ("2 warnings"), both warning messages (UserWarning: cache is stale; DeprecationWarning: old API, use v2), and one of the two warning test identities are absent.
  • The find header counts (305 vs 7,341; 415 vs 495) and the log level summary (38 errors / 1 warning vs an actual 0 / 6), detailed above.
  • Coverage caps spent on the wrong files. In the events tree, grep's 200-match display cap is spent on .venv files first in traversal order, so hits from only 1 of our 98 own-source files are shown (totals stay exact); the ripgrep equivalent shows 22 of 99. The branch listing shows 12 of 174 names with the group count stated; the workspace listing shows 50 of 144 entries with "+94 more".
  • rtk json is a schema sketch, not the data. Of 35 records it shows record 1 only, with 21 of its 26 keys — its title and venue_name among the 5 hidden — and records 2–35 elided behind "+34 more".
  • The tree footer's counts (69 directories, 144 files) dropped, and the full failing URL in the log line truncated (the failure fact itself survived).

The full item-by-item preservation report — all 127 checks, each marked found, equivalent, lost or contradicted — is kept with the experiment's records at the Experiment Desk. One method note: RTK attaches rtk recall <hash> handles for elided content. The frozen method judges the output as delivered — fetching the original back would cost extra turns and tokens, partially negating the saving — so recalled content counts as not present. The handles are a real mitigation, and they are recorded where they appear.

The battery

Asked questions, the RTK reader guessed

A small battery corroborated the preservation check: 8 questions over 3 of the largest outputs, every answer computed from the raw output beforehand, one model (Muse Wonder) reading either the raw or the RTK version. On the log payload — the one scored on both arms — the raw reader answered 3/3 and the RTK reader 2/3, missing exactly the exception cause (the "HTTPError" guess above). The battery leg therefore failed too: RTK did not answer at least as well as raw. On the find payload, RTK's reader scored 0/2 — answering "415" (its own header's wrong count; the truth is 495) and "not listed" for a file its filter had pruned; the raw arm of that payload and both arms of the ripgrep payload returned empty at the output cap, an instrument limit disclosed in the method notes, and were discarded rather than scored. Total battery spend was $0.7999 of the $1 cap.

License & method

The open-source edition only — checksum-verified

  • Binary. RTK v1.0.1, the rtk-oss edition — the official Apache-2.0 release build, downloaded from the project's GitHub release and verified against the release's own checksums file (SHA-256 matched). The tarball ships the Apache License, Version 2.0. Project documentation describes this edition as the same binary built without the BUSL-1.1 compression engine, "under Apache-2.0 alone". The default release binary, which links the BUSL-1.1 engine, was not downloaded or run; nor was the separate BUSL proxy daemon.
  • Invocation. As an agent would use it — rtk git, rtk grep, rtk find, rtk ls, rtk pytest, rtk log and friends, defaults everywhere, no configuration.
  • Counting. tiktoken 0.14.0, cl100k_base, on the exact captured bytes — the same encoder as our OpenUI and GCF tests. (RTK's own counts are bytes÷4 estimates of output tokens; ours are not supposed to match theirs.)
  • Preservation. Diagnostics were extracted mechanically from each raw output — failed-test lines, error lines, summary counts, warning lines, exception fragments, level counts, totals, content anchors — and checked against the RTK output verbatim (whitespace-normalized) or by an equivalent that keeps the name and the fact. Conservative: anything requiring a guess counts as lost.
Limitations

What this test does not show

  • The git commands ran in a clone of RTK's own upstream repository (2,527 commits), because no local workspace repo has more than 5 commits — all are shallow clones or uncommitted trees. git status ran in one of our own trees (~135 staged files).
  • The failing suite was scratch-built for this test, failing in specific named ways (a zero division, a wrong expected total, a missing key, plus two warnings), since no local real suite with failures was available. It is small and pytest-only; other runners (jest, cargo test) have their own RTK filters, untested here.
  • One corpus skew, disclosed. The events-tree commands ran at the repo root, so grep/find/rg include the repo's own .venv (7,034 of the 7,341 find results; most of the 25,241 grep matches). That is the command an agent would actually run there, but it inflates the raw bulk and makes RTK's display caps bind on third-party files first. On a venv-free tree the cuts would be smaller and the coverage fractions better.
  • The battery is small — 8 questions, one run per cell, two payloads partially unscored. It corroborates the preservation check; it does not stand alone.
  • Defaults only. rtk read was tested at its default level (passthrough); its stronger compression levels, and alternative modes like rtk err and rtk summary, are separate features an adopter would opt into, untested here.
  • Output tokens only. RTK's claim — and this test — concern command output. Nothing here measures end-to-end session cost, where the system prompt and tool definitions dominate.

Bottom line. RTK's compression is real and often dramatic, and its test-failure reporting is genuinely excellent. But the frozen claim was "60–90% with nothing an agent needs lost," and on the one output where the diagnostic was the content — the error log — RTK kept the fact of failure and discarded the cause, then summarized the log's health backwards. Adoptable, if at all, only where its summaries are known to keep what matters — test runs, listings, search totals — not as a blanket proxy for everything an agent reads, and never for logs.

Sources: the RTK repository and its v1.0.1 release (github.com/rtk-ai/rtk; the tested build is the Apache-2.0 rtk-oss edition) for the tool and its claims; the tank's own events pipeline, harvest logs and workspace for the command corpus.
